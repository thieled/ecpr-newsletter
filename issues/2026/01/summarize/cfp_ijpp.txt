Call for Papers for a Special Issue of The International Journal of Press/Politics
“An Emotional Divide: The Relationship between Media and Politics in Times of Perpetual Crisis”



Guest Editors: Lenka Waschková Císařová (Masaryk University, Czechia),

Alena Kluknavská (Masaryk University, Czechia),

Sophie Lecheler (University of Vienna, Austria),

Folker Hanusch (University of Vienna, Austria)

Deadline for Abstracts: 2 February 2026

Political communication and political journalism today operate in a climate of permanent and complex crisis, from democratic backsliding, pandemics and wars to climate emergencies and disruptive digital technologies such as AI. These complex crises do more than challenge journalism and democracy; they also transform emotions from background influences into defining forces that structure how politics and media operate.

As a growing literature in both journalism studies and political research illustrates, emotions have never been exogenous factors at the intersection of journalism and politics (Vobič et al. 2017; Ross 2010). They are integral parts of how political and journalistic actors produce information, how they disseminate it, and how they reach their audiences (Homan et al. 2023; Wahl-Jorgensen and Pantti 2021; Kotisova 2019). They also shape the relationships journalists and politicians have with each other (Van Dalen et al. 2011; Brants et al. 2010). Lastly, both journalistic and political work are structured by professional norms and practices that regulate how emotionality is displayed and strategically used, whether to secure sources or persuade voters (Schmidt 2021; Heaney 2019).

While the research literature on emotion in politics and journalism is still evolving, it has now also become clear that recent political, technological, economic and societal disruptions call for new approaches to studying the role of emotion in this context (Šimunjak 2023). In a time when multiple complex crises influence public discourse and political decision-makers, political communication and journalism are increasingly required to develop narratives that foster solutions and resilience, rather than divisions and political instrumentalization.

What is more, several current crises directly affect politics and media, such as the spread of disinformation or the rise of generative AI. In these contexts, political actors and journalists are challenged to use their norms and practices not only to solidify their continuing role within their media and political systems but also to find a balance between innovation and traditionalism. All this requires new competences, many of which involve using human emotions in appropriate and productive ways (Russmann et al. 2024). Thus, emotional change is not merely a by-product of conflict and uncertainty; it is a defining factor within them and must therefore play a central role in how we study politics and journalism going forward.

This special issue invites contributions that investigate how emotions, media and politics intersect in a time of chronic disruption. We welcome work that maps the past and present of emotionality and emotions in journalism and political communication, analyses emotions as both consequences and drivers of social and political divides, and situates these dynamics in comparative and cross-cultural contexts, asking how different democratic cultures and forms of government shape emotional styles of journalism. We are especially interested in analyses of how crises become emotional resources in politics and journalism, and with what consequences for democratic life.

Submissions may address, but are not limited to:

·       Emotions in political communication – emotional strategies in election campaigns and populist rhetoric; affective polarisation and journalism’s role within it; emotions and citizens’ trust in democratic institutions; emotional appeals in crisis communication; digital forms of emotional mobilisation.

·       Journalism and emotional storytelling – balancing neutrality and engagement; the use of fear, hope, empathy, or outrage in crisis reporting; ethical boundaries of emotional appeals.

·       Democracy and emotional publics – how emotions foster or hinder democratic participation and deliberation; emotional narratives supporting or contesting democracy; journalism’s role in cultivating empathy in polarised societies.

·       Technological change and emotions – algorithmic amplification of emotional content; AI, personalisation, and emotional manipulation; the impact of digital platforms on tone, credibility, and trust in journalism.

·       Journalists’ emotions and professional life – emotional labour, resilience, and burnout; emotional dynamics in journalist–politician interactions.

·       Norms, legitimacy, and emotion – how professional standards, democratic values, and institutional contexts regulate emotional practices in politics and journalism.

·       Comparative perspectives – differences in emotionality and emotional practices across democracies and countries; local versus global emotional narratives.

·       Wars, conflicts, and crisis reporting – emotions in wartime journalism; mobilising fear, solidarity, and hope in conflict narratives.

·       Emotions, disinformation, and conspiracy theories – emotional triggers as drivers of falsehoods; fear, outrage, and distrust in conspiracy narratives.

We welcome diverse methodologies and genres, such as theoretical and conceptual essays, historical analyses, cross-national comparisons, qualitative or quantitative studies, and mixed-method designs, as well as submissions reflecting diverse regional and national contexts.
